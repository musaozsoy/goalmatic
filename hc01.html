Chapter 7 · The letter in pencil

The envelope was on the kitchen table, propped against the sugar bowl.

It was the colour of weak tea, and soft, like cloth that has been washed too many times. There was no stamp on it and no postmark. On the front, in pencil, in handwriting that leaned forward as if it were in a hurry, it said:

Emre
The top of the house
Vine Street, Coventry
The year with the quiet sky

"It was on the mat this morning," said Babaanne. "The door was locked. The chain was on." She sat down very carefully. "I have lived in this house for forty years, and I have never had a letter like that."

Emre's hands were not quite steady as he opened it. The paper inside was soft at the folds, as if it had been opened and read a hundred times, although no one but the writer could have read it yet.

17th November 1940

Dear Emre,

We found Dad. He was in the hospital with a bandage round his head like a pudding cloth. He had been pulling a man out of a cellar in Hertford Street when the wall came down. When he saw me he laughed so hard the nurse told him off.

I have not told anyone about the attic. They would think the bombs had shaken my brains loose. But I keep thinking about your sky. I lie in the shelter and I think about the plane that was only going somewhere else. So thank you for the quiet. It helps more than you know.

Mum is sending Peggy and me to Aunt Edie's in the country until the bombing stops. I am putting the ball in the crate with my notebook, for you. Look after Peggy's crate. Peggy pretends not to be sad, so you have to listen very carefully.

Yours sincerely,
Arthur Bailey (aged 11 and three-quarters)

P.S. Don't let anyone throw the crate away. I'm not finished with it.

Emre read it out loud. Babaanne listened with her eyes closed. When he got to the part about Peggy, she pressed her hand against her mouth and did not move it for a long time.

"Peggy was exactly like that," she said at last, very quietly. "Smiling on the outside. I was the only one who knew." She took the letter and held it close to her face, reading it again slowly, as if every word were made of glass.

"He says he isn't finished," said Emre. "But he's — he'd be —"

"Ninety-seven," said Babaanne. She folded the letter along its soft lines. "That is not a boy who vanishes, Emre. That is a boy who is planning something."

Above them, very faintly, something bumped across the attic floor. It sounded exactly like a ball rolling.

Q8 · HOW · notebook · 2 marks

How does the author show that Babaanne is deeply moved by the letter?

Point: The author shows her actions – she freezes, goes quiet and handles the letter as if it is precious. Evidence: "she pressed her hand against her mouth and did not move it for a long time"; she reads it "as if every word were made of glass". HOW = what the author does (actions, a comparison) + quote it.
Q9 · WHY · notebook · 2 marks

Why does Arthur thank Emre "for the quiet"?

Point: Remembering the peaceful 2026 sky comforts him during the bombing. Evidence: Arthur says "I lie in the shelter and I think about the plane that was only going somewhere else… It helps more than you know." WHY = the character's own words.

Two Cathedrals, One City

A non-fiction text. Notice the subheadings – they tell you where to look for each answer.

Two Cathedrals, One City

Visitors to Coventry are often surprised to find not one cathedral but two, standing side by side. One is a ruin, open to the sky. The other is a modern building of red stone and glass. Together, they tell one of the most remarkable stories of the Second World War.

The night the city burned

On the night of 14th November 1940, hundreds of German bombers attacked Coventry in a raid code-named Moonlight Sonata. The attack lasted around eleven hours. More than 500 people were killed, and thousands of homes were destroyed. The medieval cathedral of St Michael was hit by fire bombs and burned through the night. By morning, only its outer walls and its tall spire were still standing.

Father Forgive

Many people were angry and wanted revenge. The cathedral's leader, Provost Richard Howard, decided on a different message. He had the words Father Forgive written on the wall of the ruined building – words that were later carved behind the altar. Two charred roof beams that had fallen in the shape of a cross were tied together and set up in the ruins. A third cross was made from three medieval nails found in the ashes. It became known as the Cross of Nails.

Choosing not to rebuild

After the war, the people of Coventry made an unusual decision. Instead of clearing the ruins away, they kept them, open to the sky, as a reminder of what war does. Next to them, a new cathedral was designed by the architect Basil Spence. It was completed in 1962.

A message for the world

Coventry became one of the first cities in the world to "twin" with another city. In 1944 it twinned with Stalingrad in Russia, and in 1959 it twinned with Dresden in Germany – a city that had itself been destroyed by British bombs. Today, copies of the Cross of Nails can be found in churches and schools all over the world, as a symbol of reconciliation.

In my opinion, it is the most moving building in Britain, and every child in Coventry should visit it at least once.

Q10 · WHY · notebook · 2 marks

Why do you think Coventry chose to keep the ruins instead of clearing them away? Use the text.

Point: They wanted people to remember the damage war causes, so it would not happen again. Evidence: the ruins were kept "open to the sky, as a reminder of what war does." Answer + quote = 2. "I think" is fine, but the proof must come from the text.
